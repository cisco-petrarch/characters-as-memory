Characters as Memory

Script reform, diaspora preservation, and the limits of Western cataloging

I. The Script as Political Act

In 1956, the newly established People's Republic of China promulgated the Scheme of Simplified Chinese Characters — standardizing 515 simplified forms, affecting thousands of compound characters. This was not a neutral act of modernization. It was ideology made legible, literacy weaponized for socialist consolidation.

The groundwork had been laid decades earlier: the May Fourth Movement (1919) had already framed classical Chinese script as a barrier to modernity; the Kuomintang attempted their own simplification list in 1935 (324 characters, ultimately withdrawn). But the PRC reform of 1956–1964 carried the weight of a state apparatus behind it. By 1977, a second round — proposing 248 additional simplified characters — was floated and then rescinded in 1986 after public backlash. The first round calcified as permanent.

Premier Zhou Enlai's 1958 speech drew the line: no Romanization, no alphabetization. Simplification was the compromise between radical script abolition (which many reformers had championed) and the preservation of the character-based writing system. The form would change; the system would survive.

II. What the Stroke Removed

Simplification was not just about reducing stroke count. It altered the internal logic of characters — the visual reasoning that allowed a reader to intuit meaning from component parts. Traditional Chinese characters operate as a semantic network: radicals (部首, bù shǒu) encode meaning categories; their position within a character signals relationship, not just phonetics.

When a radical is removed or replaced, the cultural logic encoded in the character is severed. The simplified form may still communicate the same word — but it no longer shows why.

The 214 Kangxi radicals of the traditional system are reduced to 189 in the simplified. This is not merely aesthetic loss — it is a reduction in the visual epistemology that the writing system carried. Learners of simplified Chinese encounter a script that functions, but does not narrate its own etymology.

III. The Diaspora as Living Archive

The split between simplified and traditional is not only geographic — it is temporal. Communities that emigrated before 1956, or that maintain cultural and political connections to Taiwan and Hong Kong, continue to use traditional characters as a daily practice. The script is not merely a writing system; it is a stance.

Chinatown newspapers across North America — from San Francisco's Chung Sai Yat Po (中西日報, est. 1900) to Vancouver's Chinatown News (1953–1966) to the continent-wide World Journal (美洲世界日報) — were and are printed in traditional characters. These publications constitute a parallel archive: community life, political commentary, obituaries, and advertisements, all in a script that the mainland had declared obsolete.

For Cantonese-speaking communities especially — the dominant wave of 19th and early 20th century Chinese immigration to the US — traditional characters are embedded in everyday reading practice, temple signage, merchant storefronts, and family records. The script and the dialect are intertwined: Cantonese written forms exist almost exclusively in traditional script, with no meaningful simplified parallel.

IV. The Cataloging Problem

US academic libraries catalog Chinese-language materials using MARC 21 records, OCLC WorldCat, and systems like Columbia's CLIO or Yale's Orbis. The dominant standard for romanization is Library of Congress Pinyin — a transliteration scheme based on Mandarin pronunciation, derived from mainland PRC conventions.

This creates structural erasure at several levels:

1. The romanization problem. Library of Congress Pinyin prioritizes Mandarin phonology. A Cantonese-language publication, cataloged in Pinyin, is effectively stripped of its linguistic identity. A researcher looking for Cantonese-language materials cannot search by the phonology they know. Jyutping romanization (the standard for Cantonese) is nowhere in the authority files.

2. The script disambiguation problem. WorldCat searches in traditional characters default to Taiwanese databases; simplified defaults to mainland Chinese databases. But diaspora materials — printed in traditional characters, produced in the US, about American communities — fall awkwardly between these poles. They are not Taiwanese; they are not PRC-aligned; but the catalog infrastructure assumes one axis: traditional/Taiwan or simplified/mainland.

3. The authority record problem. NACO (Name Authority Cooperative) and LCNAF (LC Name Authority File) follow LC conventions for Chinese names, which prioritize Pinyin Mandarin forms. An author who published under a Cantonese romanization of their name in 1920s San Francisco may appear in the catalog under a Mandarin rendering they never used, disconnecting the record from the community's self-documentation.

4. The subject heading problem. LCSH (Library of Congress Subject Headings) reflects the historical biases of Western knowledge organization. "Chinese Americans" as a subject heading was not established until 1976; earlier records may be filed under headings that would not be recognizable to the communities described. "Overseas Chinese" flattens enormous heterogeneity of language, politics, class, and region.

"Chinese Library Classification (CLC) is noted but not standard in US MARC systems." And CEAL workshops note a persistent "lack of expertise" in rare and specialized CJK materials — with dialect materials and diaspora community archives effectively outside the scope of existing training frameworks. — CEAL / Library of Congress, CJK cataloging documentation

The result: a vast archive of Chinese diasporic cultural production that is technically cataloged but functionally undiscoverable — misfiled under the wrong language identity, searchable only through a phonology you may not speak, and grouped with materials from a political context (mainland China, Taiwan) that may be antithetical to the community that produced them.

V. Theoretical Anchors

Cultural memory (Assmann): Jan and Aleida Assmann's distinction between communicative memory (living, generational, oral) and cultural memory (institutionally preserved, textual, codified) maps directly onto the script divide. Traditional characters, as used in diaspora communities, occupy a liminal space — they are both daily communicative practice and a form of cultural memory, a link to a pre-1956 world that the mainland has officially superseded.

Archive and counter-archive: Derrida's Archive Fever argues that the archive is always a site of power — what is preserved, how it is organized, who controls access. Community newspapers in traditional characters are counter-archival objects: they preserve a version of Chinese cultural life that the official archive (PRC-aligned databases, Mandarin-normalized catalogs) does not recognize as central.

Critical cataloging: The growing field of critical librarianship (Drabinski, Berman, Olson) examines how cataloging systems encode the epistemic frameworks of their designers. For Chinese diaspora materials, the dominant framework is doubly alienating: Western bibliographic infrastructure (MARC, AACR2, LCSH) layered over Mandarin-Pinyin romanization priorities, leaving Cantonese, Hokkien, Hakka, and Taishanese voices doubly marginal.
